Section 01

Hero Headline

[ When everything that made this organization work lived in one person's head — and now they're leaving. ]

what goes here

What belongs here

One sentence in the client's frame. Org memory work almost always enters through a transition — someone leaving, a merger, a growth moment where institutional knowledge can't scale tacitly anymore. The headline should name that precipitating event.

Not "We capture institutional knowledge." Instead: the moment when the client realizes they need to.

Section 02

Lede — who it's for

[ Two sentences. Who. What shifts. ]

what goes here

What belongs here

Who specifically — what kind of transition, what kind of organization. And the key distinction: we make tacit knowledge legible without making it lifeless. That's the tension of this work. The lede should hold it.

The failure mode of org memory work is producing a binder nobody reads. The lede should signal that's not what happens here.

Section 03

Entry Scenarios

[ 3–4 real situations — the precipitating moment. ]

what goes here

What belongs here

The situations that make organizations realize they have an org memory problem. Usually: a key person leaving, a new leader arriving and discovering there are no documents, a merger where two cultures' knowledge need to merge. Write from the moment of realization.

Best entries for this domain tend to be specific and poignant — the founding director, the ED who held everything, the key program lead. Those specifics make the scenarios land.

Section 04

Engagement Types

[ 3–4 types. What the org has at the end. ]

what goes here

What belongs here

The actual work — but named by what's produced, not the methodology. For org memory, the product is understanding and capacity, not a document. The engagement types should reflect the different forms this work takes: transition support, knowledge architecture, narrative harvest.

The distinction: what the organization can do after the work that it couldn't before. Not: "we produced a 40-page knowledge map."

Section 05

Contrast

[ What knowledge management usually produces vs. what we do. ]

what goes here

What belongs here

The conventional approach to org memory is documentation — SOPs, wikis, knowledge bases. Limicelia's approach is different: the goal is that the understanding lives in the organization, not in a document. Name that distinction honestly. Or skip this section if the market isn't crowded enough for contrast to matter.

This domain may not need a contrast section. Discuss before including.

Section 06

Related Domains

[ 2–3 domains that arise from or travel with this. ]

what goes here

What belongs here

Org Memory often connects to Leadership (the person whose memory is leaving), Org Design (the structures that hold or don't hold knowledge), and sometimes Conflict (transitions often surface old tensions). Name the real relationships, not a service tour.

The test: would a client doing org memory work naturally run into this adjacent domain? If yes, include it.

Section 07

Invitation

[ Not a pitch. An honest next step. ]

what goes here

What belongs here

Someone reading this page is probably in the middle of a transition — worried, uncertain, aware that something important could be lost. The invitation should meet that state. It should feel like the beginning of a conversation, not a sales process.

Tone: "Tell us what's at stake. We'll help you figure out what needs to be captured and what needs to be built." Not: "Schedule a knowledge audit."

Section 01

Hero Headline

"When the founding director who held everything in her head for twelve years is leaving — and month three of the new ED's tenure, there are no documents for how any of this actually works."

Section 02

Lede

For organizations navigating leadership transitions, growth moments, or mergers where what the organization knows cannot be allowed to leave with the people who currently carry it. We make tacit knowledge legible without making it lifeless — the goal is that understanding lives in the organization, not in a binder.

Section 03

Entry Scenarios

The founding director who held everything in her head for twelve years.

The new ED on month three, realizing there are no documents for how any of this actually works.

A merger where two organizations' ways of knowing need to become one — without either being flattened.

Section 04

Engagement Types

Leadership Transition Support — Accompanying an organization through the knowledge transfer embedded in a leadership change. We work with the outgoing and incoming leader together when possible — and we capture what neither would think to document.

Narrative Harvest — Drawing out organizational history, decision logic, and relational knowledge through structured conversation. The output is legible knowledge that the organization can hold and use — not a report that gets filed.

Knowledge Architecture — Designing the systems and practices through which an organization captures, stores, and activates what it learns over time. For organizations that want to build this capacity, not just address a single moment.

Section 05

Contrast

What most org knowledge work produces: Documentation. SOPs, wikis, process maps. Comprehensive, accurate, and then unread within six months because no one built the culture of using them.

What we do: Work at the level of understanding — the reasoning behind decisions, the relationships that make things work, the things that are true that nobody has written down. The goal is that the organization can think without the person who held the knowledge. That's different from capturing what they know.

Section 06

Related Domains

Organizational Design & Governance — Knowledge gaps often signal structural gaps. Sometimes the real work is building the roles and processes that carry institutional learning forward.

Leadership & Culture Transformation — Leadership transitions are rarely just about knowledge transfer — they're about identity, power, and what the organization becomes next. These domains often run together.

Section 07

Invitation

Tell us what's at stake — who's leaving, what they know, what you're worried about losing. We'll help you figure out what needs to be captured, what needs to be built, and what needs to be let go.

C / Co-Steward Prompts

Questions to answer

Work in order. Your answers become the content.

Section 01 · Hero Headline

The current headline is very specific — a founding director, twelve years, new ED, month three. Is that specificity right, or is it too narrow?

Specific headlines often land better than general ones — they give the reader permission to say "that's not us" or "that's exactly us." Is this the most common entry to this work?

Section 02 · Lede

"Make tacit knowledge legible without making it lifeless" — is that the right phrase? Does it capture what actually happens, or is it a bit precious?

If clients wouldn't recognize this language, replace it with what they would say about the difference between good and bad versions of this work.

Section 03 · Entry Scenarios

What are the actual situations you've been called into for org memory work? What made each one urgent?

Draft scenarios may be generalized. Replace with real situations, even roughly described. The urgency and specificity of real situations is more compelling than well-written fictions.

Section 04 · Engagement Types

Are the three types (Leadership Transition Support, Narrative Harvest, Knowledge Architecture) right? Is there a type of this work that's missing?

Specifically: is Knowledge Architecture (the ongoing systems work) something you're currently offering, or is it aspirational?

Section 05 · Contrast

Should the contrast section appear on this page? The org memory market isn't as crowded as org design or AI. Does a comparison add clarity or length?

Real question — not rhetorical. If it doesn't help a client make a better decision, cut it.

Overall

Is there anything on this page that implies a capability or scope that isn't currently available — or understates what is?
