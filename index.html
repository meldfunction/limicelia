Across every tradition that has tried to make sense of profound change, there is a river. And a guide who knows its currents.

The moments we walk alongside — succession and rupture, structures dissolving, cultures shifting beneath their own feet, rapid growth that outpaces what an organization knows about itself, the values reckoning that splits what came before from what must come next — these are passages. Not problems to be solved from the outside. Passages to be completed in relationship, with someone willing to stay in the water.

Every wisdom tradition has a name for this figure. The ferryman. The one who carries the lantern into the threshold. The guide who has traversed the territory and does not flinch at the dark.

What we have learned from that lineage: the guide does not carry you across. They walk alongside. And in that walking, something is built — the collective capacity to read your own currents, to hold uncertainty without collapsing it, to move together in ways that were not possible before you entered the water. The crossing itself becomes the teacher.

We stay until you can cross without us.

Most organizations face the same passage twice — because nothing changed the first time.

A passage is a specific kind of moment — when the old structure can no longer hold but the new one hasn't formed yet. Not a problem with a solution. A threshold that requires presence.

A succession. A merger. A founding team split. A values reckoning. A coalition forming. A rapid change in leadership. A restructure. A culture that shifted and never came back.

What happens at a passage depends entirely on whether anyone is present enough to help the organization complete it — not just survive it.

01 / Who we work with

When the system is at a threshold

Organizations

When a founding team splits and the culture hasn't recovered. When the equity commitment made it onto the website but not into the room. When rapid growth revealed something the strategic plan didn't anticipate. When the company has outgrown the culture that built it. When the same problem keeps coming back.

Nonprofits · Cooperatives · Foundations · B-Corps · Social Enterprise

Institutions & civic systems

When the institution has made a public commitment — to equity, to modernization, to a different kind of service — and the internal culture hasn't caught up. When a technology adoption is failing not because of the technology. When what the agency says it does and what the people inside it experience every day have quietly come apart.

Government Agencies · Civic Institutions · Public Sector · Anchor Institutions

Communities & networks

When a shared purpose exists but shared governance doesn't yet. When a coalition, mutual aid network, or practitioner field has grown beyond what informal trust can hold. When the work is to build enough common ground that movement becomes possible — before anyone agrees on what that movement is.

Mutual Aid Networks · Coalitions · Place-based Communities · Practitioner Fields · Movement Organizations

When the people are at a threshold

Leaders at their own threshold

Sometimes the most significant passage an organization faces is the one its leader is walking through. When what's being asked of you has outgrown the person you were when you stepped into the role. When the thing you built has become the thing you're navigating from inside. We walk alongside — not to optimize performance, but to accompany what's actually happening.

Executive Directors · Founders · Emerging Leaders · Co-founders

The People doing this work

Teams, units, and communities carrying the work of change together. When the people doing the work are giving more than they're receiving from it — across a team, a coalition, a field. When the capacity to facilitate, hold conflict, or sustain community is depleting faster than it's being restored. We work with the collective, not just the individual.

Teams · Coalitions · Facilitation Networks · Community Orgs · Capacity Builders

04 / Lineage

We stand on
many shoulders.

Our practice draws from participatory, governance, and wisdom traditions built by others over decades. These are among the most significant — not a complete account. We name them because attribution is an act of integrity, not courtesy.

Image by DEZALB from Pixabay

Art of Hosting A living network of practices for hosting conversations that matter — including World Café, Open Space Technology, and Circle Practice. artofhosting.org
Sociocracy Socius (Latin: companion) + kratia (Greek: rule) — governance by those in relationship with each other. Developed by Gerard Endenburg in the Netherlands in the 1970s, drawing from cybernetics: the science of self-regulating systems through feedback loops. Endenburg applied that thinking to human organizations — how groups can steer themselves without hierarchy. sociocracy.info
Systems Convening A role and a practice: nurturing learning and connection across the boundaries of a landscape. Etienne and Beverly Wenger-Trayner. systemsconvening.net
Warm Data Transcontextual information about the interrelationships that integrate a complex system. International Bateson Institute / Nora Bateson. warmdatalab.net
Deep Democracy Arnold Mindell's process work — attending to the voices at the margins of a system, the ones not speaking in the meeting but shaping everything. The work is with what a group is avoiding as much as what it's engaging.
Popular Education Paulo Freire and the tradition of learning that starts from people's own experience and context. The knowledge is already in the room. The work is creating conditions for it to become visible and actionable. A foundation of how Colmenar holds its work.
Wisdom & contemplative traditions Across many lineages — including Vedic, somatic, and contemplative practice traditions — the quality of a guide's attention is not incidental to the work. It is the work.

These traditions are living. We are practitioners within them, not their authors — and other lineages continue to shape this practice. We continue to learn.

05 / How we hold the work

Every engagement follows the same arc: we listen from the frontline up before we speak with leadership. We name what we find honestly — what's working and what the structure is producing that no one wants to name. We stay through the co-design and the rebuilding, not just the diagnosis. The goal is always to leave capacity, not dependency.

The stewards' own practice — contemplative, relational, across years of working inside organizations at moments of genuine difficulty — is not incidental to this. It is what makes accompaniment possible. You cannot guide someone through territory you have not traversed.

We operate as a nonprofit practice. Consulting revenue funds gift-based community offerings that carry no fee. We price on a sliding scale based on what an organization can sustain. Fees are discussed after we understand what the situation calls for — say early if budget is a real constraint.
