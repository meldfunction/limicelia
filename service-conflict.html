Section 01

Hero Headline

[ When the rupture nobody will name has become the thing everyone is working around. ]

what goes here

What belongs here

One sentence in the client's frame. Conflict work entries are often about avoidance — the thing that's happening but not being named. The headline should name it without sensationalizing it.

The test: does it make someone think "that's exactly what's happening with us"? If yes, it's working.

Section 02

Lede — who it's for

[ Two sentences. Who. What shifts. ]

what goes here

What belongs here

Who specifically calls about conflict — what kind of organization, what kind of rupture. And what becomes possible after the work. The test: does "clarity" appear? Conflict work moves toward clarity, not comfort. That distinction matters.

"We don't mediate toward comfort — we move toward clarity." That's the Limicelia distinction in this domain.

Section 03

Entry Scenarios

[ 3–4 cards. The situation as they describe it, not as it's diagnosed. ]

what goes here

What belongs here

Conflict entries are specific and often charged. Avoid generic descriptions. The scenarios should name real dynamics — factions, named parties won't talk, board/staff fracture, coalition formed around shared values now fracturing around money or power.

Best source: how clients described the situation in the first five minutes of contact. Not their theory of the conflict — what they said was happening.

Section 04

Engagement Types

[ 3–4 types. Named by what they produce, not the method. ]

what goes here

What belongs here

The actual work — named by outcome, not process. Conflict work often has distinct types: rupture repair, cross-sector tension navigation, coalition formation, board-staff fracture. Each has a different entry and a different destination.

Do not list every method (NVC, restorative circles). List what the client is getting — and note that power differentials are named, not managed away.

Section 05

Contrast

[ What mediation usually does vs. what we do. ]

what goes here

What belongs here

The mediation market often moves toward agreement as a proxy for resolution. Limicelia's approach is different: clarity first, even when clarity is uncomfortable. Name that honestly. The contrast earns its place if the distinction matters to how a client chooses a practitioner.

Does this distinction matter enough to name? Yes — because clients who want comfortable resolution will be disappointed. Better to name it upfront.

Section 06

Related Domains

[ 2–3 domains that travel with conflict work. ]

what goes here

What belongs here

Conflict often surfaces governance gaps (Org Design) or leadership failures (Leadership & Culture). Sometimes both. Sometimes the conflict is really a coalition that hasn't formed yet (Field Building). Name the real relationships — not a tour of services.

The test: would a client in the middle of conflict work naturally encounter this adjacent domain? If yes, include it.

Section 07

Invitation

[ Not a pitch. An honest next step. ]

what goes here

What belongs here

Someone reading this page is likely in the middle of something difficult and hasn't told many people. The invitation should acknowledge that — it should feel like a door they can walk through without having to explain everything first.

Tone: "Tell us what's happening. You don't have to have it figured out." Not: "Ready to resolve your conflict? Book a call."

Hero Headline

Section 02

Lede

For organizations navigating conflict, cross-sector tension, and coalition fracture where power differentials are real and need to be named — not managed. We don't mediate toward comfort. We move toward clarity, even when clarity is hard.

Section 03

Entry Scenarios

Two departments that haven't spoken honestly in eighteen months.

A coalition held together by shared values but fracturing on who holds the money.

A rupture nobody will name in a meeting but everyone talks about in parking lots afterward.

Section 04

Engagement Types

Rupture Repair — Facilitated process for naming what happened, understanding how it happened, and deciding what to build from what remains. We don't paper over ruptures. We help organizations move through them with as much honesty as people can hold.

Cross-Sector Tension Navigation — For coalitions, partnerships, or multi-stakeholder processes where the parties share a goal but not a framework for how decisions get made. We bring the power dynamics into the room, not around it.

Coalition Formation — Designing the conditions for durable collaboration between organizations that have reason to work together but haven't yet built the relational infrastructure to do it.

Section 05

Contrast

What most conflict mediation does: Moves toward agreement. Smooths over power differentials in favor of getting to yes. Produces a document that doesn't survive first contact with reality.

What we do: Name what's actually happening — including who holds power and how that's shaping the conflict. Not every conflict resolves. But every conflict can be navigated with more clarity than it currently has. That's what we're after.

Section 06

Related Domains

Organizational Design & Governance — Conflict often surfaces where governance is unclear. Sometimes fixing the structure resolves what looked like a relational problem.

Leadership & Culture Transformation — Some conflicts are symptoms of a leadership gap. Naming that doesn't mean blaming — it means knowing what needs to change.

Section 07

Invitation

You don't have to have it figured out before you reach out. Tell us what's happening — even roughly. We'll tell you whether this is the kind of work we do, and if so, what an entry point might look like.

C / Co-Steward Prompts

Questions to answer
together.

Your answers become the content. Some questions will surface a real disagreement — that's what they're for.

Section 01 · Hero Headline

What do clients actually say when they describe why they're calling about conflict? What's the first sentence?

Often it starts with what the conflict is NOT — "we're not sure if this rises to the level of needing outside help." That's the entry. The headline should name what they're circling around.

Section 02 · Lede

"We move toward clarity, not comfort" — is that language clients would recognize as meaningful, or is it a practitioner distinction that doesn't land for them?

If clients don't understand what that means until after they've done the work, it's not lede language. It's something else.

Section 03 · Entry Scenarios

What are the two or three most common situations you've actually been called into for conflict work? Describe the call, not the diagnosis.

Drafts are written in Limicelia voice. Replace with real situations. If you have a first-call note or intake email that captures it well, use that language.

Section 03 · Entry Scenarios

Is there a conflict situation you've been called into that surprised you — one that didn't look like a "conflict" from outside?

Those are the most useful scenarios. They signal to the right clients that you understand what's really happening.

Section 04 · Engagement Types

Are the three types (Rupture Repair, Cross-Sector Tension, Coalition Formation) the right scope? What's missing? What overstates the current offer?

List only what you're currently able and genuinely willing to take on.

Section 05 · Contrast

Is the contrast with conventional mediation accurate? Does it create the right signal for the right clients — and the right filter for the wrong ones?

The contrast section should attract clients who want what you actually offer, and discourage those who want something else. Is it doing that?

Overall

Is there anything on this page that describes work you've done once but aren't ready to offer consistently? Or anything that understates what you're genuinely able to do?

Both are real risks. Overstating creates commitments you can't meet. Understating leaves the right clients confused about whether to call.
